# From NTN to Heterogeneous NTN: Architecture and Challenges

>[!NOTE]
> Author: Wei, Chang
> Date: 2026/08/31

## 1. Why Do We Need Heterogeneous NTN?

Mobile networks have traditionally relied on terrestrial base stations, fiber or microwave backhaul, and fixed core-network infrastructure. This architecture works very well in cities and other densely populated areas. However, its limitations become increasingly visible when connectivity must be extended to mountains, oceans, deserts, remote rural areas, or disaster sites.

A simple example illustrates the problem. Providing stable 5G coverage everywhere requires base stations, power, backhaul, and maintenance systems. Terrain, site acquisition, energy supply, construction time, and operating cost can be more difficult to solve than the radio technology itself. In large events or disaster-response scenarios, demand may also appear suddenly, while fixed infrastructure cannot always be deployed quickly enough.

This motivates the use of communication platforms beyond the ground. Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) extend network coverage through airborne or spaceborne platforms, complementing terrestrial networks where coverage, deployment speed, or disaster resilience is insufficient.

3GPP treats NTN as an extension of the 5G system. Release 17 introduced normative support for NR NTN and satellite access, covering radio interfaces, 5G Core architecture, procedures, and the effects of long propagation delay, Doppler shift, and platform movement. The overall standardization context is summarized in the [3GPP NTN Overview](https://www.3gpp.org/technologies/ntn-overview).

However, adding NTN does not make every networking problem disappear. A single platform is usually effective only under a particular set of conditions. The next logical step is to combine multiple platforms and select the most suitable one according to location, service requirements, and network state.

In this article, **Heterogeneous NTN**, or **H-NTN**, refers to a research architecture that integrates UAVs, HAPS, satellites, and traditional terrestrial networks (TN) as a multi-layer system for access, backhaul, and edge services. This is an architectural and research term; not every UAV deployment should automatically be considered a 3GPP NTN deployment.

## 2. What Can Terrestrial Networks and NTN Solve?

### 2.1 Limitations of Terrestrial Networks

Terrestrial networks offer mature technology, low latency, scalable capacity, and well-established spectrum and operational procedures. Nevertheless, several situations make terrestrial deployment expensive or impractical:

- **Difficult geographical coverage:** Population density is often low in mountains, at sea, in deserts, and near borders, making the return on investment of fixed base stations uncertain.
- **Dependence on infrastructure:** Base stations require reliable power and backhaul. When fiber, electricity, or roads are damaged, the radio equipment may remain operational but the service can still be unavailable.
- **Rapidly changing demand:** Disaster recovery, large events, and temporary work sites may need additional capacity within a short time. Fixed base stations are not always fast enough to deploy.
- **Coverage-capacity trade-off:** Wide coverage generally requires fewer high-power nodes, while high capacity requires denser deployment. It is difficult to optimize both at the same time.

This does not mean that TN will be replaced by NTN. A more useful view is that TN provides a stable foundation with high capacity and low latency, while NTN supplements areas where terrestrial deployment is uneconomical, difficult, or temporarily unavailable.

### 2.2 What NTN Can Add

NTN can place access or relay equipment on satellites, aircraft, or high-altitude platforms, reducing the dependence on ground infrastructure. Typical applications include broadband connectivity in remote regions, maritime and aviation connectivity, disaster recovery, temporary coverage, and additional backhaul paths for terrestrial networks.

Satellites can provide wide-area coverage across difficult terrain. HAPS can cover a large area with lower propagation delay than many satellite links. UAVs can be deployed to a specific location to provide temporary capacity for a hotspot or disaster area. These platforms are complementary; they are not simply interchangeable versions of the same network.

## 3. Why Is a Single NTN Platform Not Enough?

Different platforms have different physical environments, mobility patterns, energy constraints, and network roles. Choosing only one platform usually changes the question from “Is coverage available?” to a different set of limitations.

| Platform | Main strengths | Typical limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UAV | Fast deployment, mobility toward demand hotspots, suitable for temporary coverage | Limited battery and payload, short flight time, weather and regulatory constraints |
| HAPS | Large coverage, lower latency than many satellite links, useful for aerial backhaul | Station keeping, energy balance, equipment cost, and deployment complexity |
| LEO / MEO / GEO satellite | Wide coverage for oceans and remote regions | Propagation delay, Doppler, visibility time, satellite capacity, gateways, and feeder-link constraints |
| TN | Low latency, mature capacity planning and operations | Geographic, power, backhaul, construction, and maintenance constraints |

The term HAPS is used in several different contexts, so its definition should be checked against the relevant document. ITU Radio Regulations describe HAPS as radio stations located on an object at approximately 20–50 km altitude and maintained at a nominally fixed position relative to the Earth. ITU also identifies access and backhaul as HAPS applications; see the [ITU HAPS overview](https://www.itu.int/en/mediacentre/backgrounders/Pages/High-altitude-platform-systems.aspx).

These differences explain the motivation for H-NTN: different platforms should cooperate at the right time and place instead of expecting one platform to simultaneously provide low latency, wide coverage, high capacity, low energy consumption, and high reliability.

## 4. A Basic Heterogeneous NTN Architecture

In an H-NTN system, a UE may observe TN cells, UAV cells, HAPS cells, and satellite beams at the same time. These access points may share one 5G Core, or they may belong to different operators and management domains. User traffic may reach the UPF and Data Network through terrestrial fiber, microwave, UAV relays, HAPS backhaul, satellite feeder links, or inter-satellite links.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    UE["UE / IoT device / vehicle / vessel"]
    TN["TN gNB"]
    UAV["UAV access or relay"]
    HAPS["HAPS access or backhaul"]
    SAT["Satellite beam or payload"]
    GW["Gateway and transport network"]
    CORE["5G Core: AMF / SMF / PCF / NWDAF"]
    UPF["UPF and session anchor"]
    DN["Data Network and edge cloud"]

    UE --> TN
    UE --> UAV
    UE --> HAPS
    UE --> SAT
    TN --> GW
    UAV --> GW
    HAPS --> GW
    SAT --> GW
    GW --> CORE
    CORE --> UPF
    UPF --> DN
```

From the free5GC perspective, this architecture involves at least three layers:

1. **Access and mobility management:** AMF must handle registration, reachability, and mobility events across different access types.
2. **Session and user plane management:** SMF and UPF must account for path changes, session anchors, edge UPFs, and changing backhaul conditions.
3. **Policy and analytics:** PCF, NSSF, or NWDAF can provide service requirements, slice information, load status, and predictions to support platform and path selection.

The main difficulty is that access selection, resource allocation, backhaul selection, and UPF placement are no longer independent decisions. For example, a satellite beam with the strongest signal may have a congested feeder link. A nearby UAV may provide excellent radio quality but soon become unavailable because of limited battery capacity. Optimizing only one layer does not necessarily optimize the end-to-end service.

## 5. New Problems Created by Integration

### 5.1 Cell Selection: The Strongest Signal Is Not Always the Best Cell

In a conventional TN, cell selection often relies on RSRP, RSRQ, SINR, load, and cell priorities. In H-NTN, candidate cells have different temporal and spatial characteristics:

- A satellite beam may be approaching the end of its visibility window.
- A UAV's position, remaining energy, and mission may change.
- A HAPS may offer better latency and capacity than a satellite, but may not have enough available bandwidth.
- A TN signal may be weaker while its backhaul and UPF path remain more stable.

Cell selection should therefore move from a single-time-point signal comparison to a multi-objective decision with temporal prediction. Candidate scoring may include latency, expected visibility time, remaining capacity, energy, backhaul availability, service requirements, and operator policy. The algorithm must also avoid **ping-pong** behavior: rapidly alternating metrics can cause frequent switching, increasing signaling and service interruption.

### 5.2 Resource Allocation: Resources Are More Than PRBs

Resource allocation in H-NTN may jointly involve:

- Radio spectrum, PRBs, beams, and transmit power.
- UAV battery, flight time, and payload capacity.
- HAPS energy, platform capacity, and backhaul bandwidth.
- Satellite beams, feeder links, and inter-satellite-link capacity.
- Gateway, UPF, and edge-computing resources.

This makes resource allocation a cross-layer problem. Assigning many UEs to one platform affects the radio scheduler, the backhaul queue, and the core-network path at the same time. Throughput, fairness, latency, energy consumption, reliability, and service priority can all conflict with one another.

### 5.3 Mobility and Handover: The UE Is Not the Only Moving Entity

Terrestrial handover is mainly driven by UE movement and neighboring-cell relationships. In H-NTN, cells, beams, satellites, UAVs, and backhaul paths may all move or change state. A transition may occur:

- Between beams on the same satellite.
- Between different satellites.
- Between a UAV, HAPS, and TN.
- Without changing the access point, while the gateway, feeder link, or UPF path changes.

This raises a fundamental question: what is the unit of handover? Is it a radio cell, an access platform, a gateway, or a user-plane route? Each answer creates different signaling and state-synchronization requirements for the AMF, SMF, UPF, RAN, and transport network.

H-NTN handover also needs prediction inputs such as satellite ephemeris, UE location, UAV trajectory, weather, and backhaul availability. An incorrect prediction can trigger an unnecessary early switch, while a late switch can cause handover failure, packet loss, or PDU Session interruption.

### 5.4 Backhaul Selection: Choosing the Access Point Is Not Enough

After a UE connects to a platform, traffic still needs a path to the 5G Core or an edge cloud. Possible backhaul options include terrestrial fiber, microwave, HAPS relays, satellite feeder links, and inter-satellite links. These paths differ in latency, capacity, cost, reliability, and expected availability.

Backhaul selection should not rely only on the shortest path or the current RTT. It should also consider future availability and queue evolution. This decision is coupled with UPF selection, session anchoring, local breakout, and traffic steering. A path with excellent radio quality but poor backhaul may be worse than a path with slightly weaker radio quality and a nearby edge UPF.

### 5.5 Cross-platform Coordination: How Should Platforms Share State?

Cross-platform coordination is one of the most fundamental H-NTN problems. Different platforms may use different control periods, operators, and data models. Satellite orbit information may be updated at a seconds-to-minutes scale, while UAV energy and trajectory can change more rapidly. 5GC sessions and policies have their own lifecycles.

The shared state may include platform position and predicted trajectory, coverage, load, energy, link status, service priority, slice requirements, gateway availability, UPF availability, and handover candidates. If too little state is exchanged, decisions become stale. If too much state is exchanged, control traffic and synchronization overhead increase.

When platforms belong to different operators, additional issues arise: trust, authorization, data privacy, cross-domain SLAs, and responsibility for failures. These problems cannot be solved by a better scheduler alone.

## 6. How Can These Decisions Be Formulated?

At each time step, candidate platforms and paths can be represented as decision variables. For example, a UE may need to select a platform p, a backhaul route r, and a UPF f. The objective can minimize latency, packet loss, energy consumption, and switching cost while maximizing availability and service satisfaction:

\[
\min \; \alpha D + \beta L + \gamma E + \delta H + \epsilon C - \eta A
\]

Here, D is delay, L is packet-loss rate, E is energy consumption, H is handover cost, C is coordination or operational cost, and A is availability. The model can also include constraints for coverage, capacity, power, battery, spectrum, backhaul, and SLA requirements.

The purpose of this formulation is not to claim that one set of weights is always correct. It forces the researcher to state clearly:

- Is the objective UE experience, platform energy, or total network cost?
- Is the decision made for one instant, or must it guarantee service continuity over a future time window?
- Is the information from every platform complete and fresh?
- Can the algorithm finish before a handover or link failure occurs?

Without these definitions, simply reporting the highest throughput may hide the system-level trade-offs that make H-NTN difficult.

## 7. Open Research Questions

Several research directions remain important:

1. **Predictive access and mobility management:** How can orbit, location, traffic, energy, weather, and backhaul predictions be combined for cell selection and handover? How should the system degrade safely when predictions are wrong?
2. **Joint access, backhaul, and UPF optimization:** How can we avoid optimizing the radio, backhaul, and session anchor independently and then obtaining a poor end-to-end path?
3. **Multi-connectivity and make-before-break:** Can maintaining two platform connections during a transition reduce interruption? Are the additional power, spectrum, and packet-reordering costs acceptable?
4. **Cross-domain coordination:** How can different operators exchange the minimum sufficient state without exposing sensitive information?
5. **Network slicing and SLAs:** Should different slices use different platform-selection, backhaul, and reliability policies? Should slice isolation extend to beams, gateways, backhaul, and UPFs?
6. **Energy and sustainability:** How should UAV batteries, HAPS solar energy, and satellite power limits become first-class decision parameters instead of after-the-fact statistics?
7. **Security and resilience:** How do spoofing, jamming, false location information, malicious platforms, delayed control signaling, and cross-domain trust failures affect selection, handover, and routing?
8. **Reproducible evaluation benchmarks:** The research community needs shared mobility traces, link models, traffic profiles, baselines, KPIs, and failure scenarios to compare different approaches fairly.

## 8. Conclusion

NTN extends connectivity to places that are difficult for terrestrial networks to serve. However, after UAVs, HAPS, satellites, and TN are combined into one system, the key question is no longer simply “Which platform has the strongest signal?” Platform position, mobility, energy, capacity, backhaul, core-network state, and service requirements jointly determine the best connection for a UE.

The central H-NTN research question can be summarized as follows:

**How can an end-to-end path remain reliable, affordable, and sustainable when the available aerial, space, and terrestrial resources are dynamic and only partially observable?**

For free5GC, H-NTN highlights the need to coordinate registration, PDU Session continuity, policy control, UPF selection, and path switching as access platforms and backhaul paths change. Maintaining this coordination is a core challenge when terrestrial and non-terrestrial connectivity are integrated with the 5GC.
